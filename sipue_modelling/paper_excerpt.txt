Abstract
Data centers are rapidly becoming one of the most energy-intensive components of Europe's digital infrastructure. Although often treated as large electricity consumers, they can also function as local energy-system resources when waste heat and controllable demand are integrated with surrounding networks. Heat export, demand response, and storage-enabled grid services allow data centers to contribute to efficiency and decarbonization objectives.

This paper contributes in two parts. First, it synthesizes evidence from policy, market, and project sources on how energy characteristics and policy frameworks shape local business impacts across Europe, with a focus on investment readiness and system integration. Second, it introduces System-Integrated PUE (siPUE), a metric that adjusts PUE using construction-delay-adjusted avoided independent system cost relative to a no-services counterfactual. Heat recovery, flexibility, carbon damage, peak capacity, transmission, and resource-cost effects enter through this counterfactual system-cost term rather than through private heat or flexibility revenue. Cooling electricity is included in facility energy, while heat recovered from IT and cooling circuits can create system value when delivered as useful heat.

The metric is embedded in a two-stage system-integrated planning framework. The code reports planning objective cost, integrated planning surplus, delay factor, and independent system-benefit accounting, while ex-post settlement fields are retained only for audit. Current annual representative-day validation separates planning-objective alignment, normalized external independent system-cost validity, and absolute system-impact reporting. In the current canonical run, PUE worsens by 0.309% on average, while siPUE improves by 24.961%. siPUE strongly ranks normalized independent system benefit per unit IT energy. Absolute independent system benefit is reported separately as a scale-of-impact measure. Carbon outcomes are reported through CUE, CO2 cost, DC CO2 share, carbon damage, and the independent system-cost counterfactual used by siPUE.

1. Introduction
Electricity demand from European data centers has become a material
planning variable for both transmission and distribution networks. Commis-
sion estimates put EU consumption at 76.8 TWh in 2018, with projections
of 98.5 TWh by 2030 under a trend scenario and up to 160 TWh under a
4

high-growth case [1]. More recent assessments suggest consumption already
reached roughly 96 TWh in 2024 and could approach 168 TWh by 2030 [2].
Growth is reinforced by traffic expansion and a shift toward compute-
intensive workloads. Artificial intelligence (AI) workloads are expected to
accelerate demand, with energy needs rising rapidly during 2023–2030 [3, 4].
If these trajectories persist, the data-center share of EU electricity could
increase from about 3.1% to around 5.7% by 2035 [2], making aggregate de-
mand comparable to national consumption in several member states [5, 6].
At that scale, the build-out becomes a business and investment problem un-
der binding local constraints: connection rights and congestion on the power
side, offtake and tariff interfaces on the heat side, eligibility rules for flexi-
bility markets, and land-use approvals, permitting timelines, and community
acceptance at the site level increasingly shape which projects can proceed and
how quickly capital can be deployed [7]. Integration features can partially
mitigate these frictions by delivering local co-benefits (e.g., heat export) or
by reducing peak stress through controllability and storage, but they require
additional interfaces and investment that must be evaluated on a comparable
basis.
In parallel, the EU energy transition is pushing electricity and heat sys-
tems toward higher renewable shares, electrification, and sector coupling [8].
Policy packages emphasize digitalization and flexibility as enabling layers for
this transition [9], while sustainability reporting frameworks for large data
centers are being formalized through Directive (EU) 2023/1791 and industry
initiatives [10, 11]. This setting creates a dual role for data centers: treated
as inflexible loads they can exacerbate congestion, but when designed for
integration they can supply valuable services such as waste-heat export and
grid support [12, 13, 14, 15].
Data-center participation in heat and flexibility markets is governed by
tariffs, market access rules, and contract structures. Yet the dominant re-
porting metric, PUE, evaluates only internal electricity overhead and does
not account for externally valued services. As a result, integration invest-
ments that increase electricity use to deliver heat or flexibility can worsen
reported efficiency even when they improve economic and system outcomes.
This creates a reporting–investment tension that motivates the development
of a PUE-compatible metric capable of internalizing monetized system ser-
vices.

1.1. Contributions
This paper makes four contributions:
1. It integrates evidence on data centers and local energy systems, em-
phasizing the mechanisms through which energy characteristics shape
local business impacts and investment readiness
2. ItdefinesSystem-IntegratedPowerUsageEffectiveness(siPUE),asystem-
oriented metric that extends PUE by converting no-services avoided independent
system cost into an energy-equivalent credit, with conventional PUE recovered
in the zero-services case; carbon is also evaluated through CUE, carbon-market
cost, and independent carbon-damage diagnostics
3. It formulates a two-stage system-integrated planning model with min-max regret, explicit planning objective cost, integrated planning surplus, and independent system-benefit accounting under operational uncertainty, land costs, and construction-delay risk.
4. It quantifies how robust design choices affect PUE, siPUE, independent system benefit, and integrated planning surplus across ten services-active scenarios using annual representative-day evaluation, and reports sensitivity to CO2, heat, flexibility, congestion, land price, and construction delay.1.2. Theliteraturerelevanttothisworkspansfourmajorareas: (i)datacentre

1.2.1. Foundation of Data Centre Metrics (2008–2012)
The first generation of data centre metrics was established through The
Green Grid between 2008 and 2012, culminating in the widely adopted Power
Usage Effectiveness (PUE) metric [16, 17]. PUE provided a simple ratio of
total facility energy to IT energy and played a crucial role in raising aware-
ness about infrastructure efficiency. However, its design implicitly assumed
that (i) all non-IT energy is undesirable, (ii) energy source differences are
irrelevant, and (iii) time-varying system behaviour does not matter. These
assumptions became problematic as data centres began interacting with elec-
tricity markets, district heating networks, and flexibility services.
